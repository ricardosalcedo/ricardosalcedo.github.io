I recently read Dan Koe's viral essay *How to Fix Your Entire Life in 1 Day*. It's a self-improvement piece, not a tech article, but the core ideas map directly to problems I see every day in engineering organizations, across every role.

Here's what stuck with me, and why I think this framework matters whether you're an SDE, a PM, a TPM, a scientist, or an ops lead.

## Stop Trying to Fix Everything at Once

Koe's first point is simple: pick one future. Most people fail because they try to overhaul every dimension of their life simultaneously.

Sound familiar? Every planning cycle starts the same way. You look at your goals doc and want to ship a major feature, reduce operational load by 40%, mentor two people, write a strategy doc for next year, and learn a new skill on the side. By week three, you've made shallow progress on all of them and deep progress on none.

The people I admire most in tech, regardless of role, are the ones who pick one thing and go unreasonably deep. They become *the* person for that thing. Not because they ignore everything else, but because they know that focus compounds in a way that scattered effort never does.

A PM who goes deep on one customer problem will outperform one who spreads across ten. An SDE who masters one system will have more impact than one who skims five. A TPM who owns one critical launch end-to-end will grow faster than one who "helps out" on several. Depth is the cheat code.

## Identity First, Actions Second

The idea that resonated most was Koe's distinction between first-order and second-order change. Second-order change is forcing yourself to do the right actions: grinding through reviews, writing docs because you're supposed to, attending office hours because your manager suggested it. First-order change is becoming the kind of person who naturally does those things.

I've felt this gap. There were stretches where I forced myself to write thorough design docs, and it felt like pulling teeth. The shift happened when I stopped thinking "I need to write this doc" and started thinking "I'm the kind of engineer who thinks in writing before building." The output looked the same. The effort didn't.

This applies everywhere in engineering culture. We talk about company values as behaviors. But behaviors that come from identity stick. Behaviors that come from compliance fade the moment pressure hits, and pressure always hits.

"Disagree and commit" looks completely different when it's something you perform versus something you *are*. The person who identifies as someone who speaks hard truths doesn't need to psych themselves up before a review meeting. They just speak.

## The Anti-Vision

Koe uses what he calls an "anti-vision": vividly imagining the future you'll get if you change nothing. It's uncomfortable by design.

For any engineer, the anti-vision might look like this: it's two years from now. You're still firefighting the same problems, still saying "I'll get to that next quarter," still waiting for the perfect time to work on the thing that actually excites you. Your scope hasn't grown. Your skills haven't deepened. You're comfortable, but you're coasting. And the gap between where you are and where you could be has quietly become a canyon.

I don't think fear is a great long-term motivator. But as a one-time wake-up call? It works. Sometimes you need to stare at the default path to realize you don't want to be on it.

## Your First Two Hours Build Everything

Koe argues that the first two hours of your morning construct your life over time. Small, consistent blocks of focused work accumulate into something massive.

In engineering, the equivalent is protecting your first deep-work block. Before Slack lights up, before the first ticket escalation, before standup. That window is where the highest-leverage work happens. The design thinking. The complex analysis. The writing that actually moves the needle. The strategic work that never feels urgent but determines everything.

When I protect that block, my weeks feel productive. When I let it get eaten by reactive work, I end the week wondering where the time went. Same number of hours. Completely different output.

This isn't just an engineering thing. A PM who spends their first two hours on customer research instead of email will write sharper specs. A TPM who uses that block for risk analysis instead of status updates will catch problems before they become escalations. The principle is universal: protect the time where you do your best thinking.

## What I'm Taking From This

This isn't about a blog post changing your life. It's about choosing a frame and committing to it:

1. **Pick one thing this half.** Not three. One. Go so deep that people associate your name with it. For me right now, that's FPGA debugging workflows, not because other things don't matter, but because depth here compounds into everything else.
2. **Think identity, not tasks.** Don't just do the work. Decide who you are. The tasks follow. "I'm someone who writes before building" is more durable than "I need to write this doc."
3. **Protect the morning block.** Two hours of focused work before the chaos starts. Non-negotiable. This is where your career is actually built.
4. **Stare at the default path.** Once. Honestly. Then choose a different one.

If you haven't read Koe's essay, it's worth the 15 minutes. Not because it tells you anything new, but because sometimes the obvious thing, said at the right time, is exactly what breaks you out of autopilot.
